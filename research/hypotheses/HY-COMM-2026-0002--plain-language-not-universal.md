# Hypothesis

## Statement

Plain-language practices often improve accessibility, comprehension, or satisfaction, but their effect depends on audience, baseline, content, and outcome; they cannot serve as a universal communication objective.

## Mechanism

Reducing unnecessary lexical and syntactic burden can free cognitive resources, but oversimplification can remove precision, and unchanged information structure or missing concepts can remain limiting.

## Predictions

- Plain-language variants will show heterogeneous effects across source documents.
- Accessibility or satisfaction gains will not always imply comprehension gains.
- Audience expertise and the adequacy of underlying information will moderate effects.

## Evidence that would support it

Controlled studies with heterogeneous effects, metric divergence, or cases where another representation outperforms plain wording.

## Evidence that would contradict it

Broad, replicated evidence that a stable set of plain-language transformations improves all decision-relevant outcomes across audiences without meaningful tradeoffs.

## Tests performed

Compared one randomized trial, one systematic review, and accessibility guidance.

## Results

Supported at High confidence within written informational communication; broader cross-cultural and technical-domain testing remains.

## Falsification attempts

The randomized trial showed a large comprehension benefit for one item, but a small nonsignificant benefit for another while satisfaction improved in both.

## Current assessment

Plain language is a candidate intervention and accessibility constraint, not the project’s master theory.

## Next experiment

Factor wording separately from information structure in a procedural task and measure both performance and subjective ease.
