# Executive Summary

This REP expands the literary corpus from seven predominantly white male U.S.
figures to 29 additional writers across women Beats, Harlem Renaissance, Black
Arts and Black feminist work, five nation-specific Indigenous contexts,
immigrant/diasporic/queer writing, and six non-U.S. traditions. The durable
result is not a larger menu of styles. It is a boundary: communication patterns
derive force partly from situated authority—community, language, history,
relationships, land, and access to culturally governed material. Functions may
transfer; persona and authority do not [EV-COMM-2026-6A27 through
EV-COMM-2026-A4F7; HY-COMM-2026-F19E].

The reading guide gives an entry work, deeper works, and a function to observe
for every writer. Confidence is Medium (0.66 judgment): bibliographic and
functional distinctions converge, while original-language analysis, community
review, and audience outcomes remain absent.

# Current Understanding

Literary form can enact audience position: vernacular can create access or
refuse assimilation; multilingualism can expose partial understanding; montage
can preserve historical fracture; oral-written braiding can maintain relations
among land, family, nation, and archive; manifesto can mobilize while also
excluding; lyric intimacy can scale into public ethics. These functions cannot
be evaluated apart from who speaks, who is addressed, what history structures
the exchange, and what material may circulate.

# Key Discoveries

1. Women Beats replace the masculine spontaneity default with distinct
   programmatic, diaristic, maternal, exilic, and performance practices
   [EV-COMM-2026-6A27].
2. Harlem and Black Arts lineages contain incompatible strategies—formal
   compression, vernacular accessibility, montage, folklore, manifesto,
   neighborhood observation, and intersectional critique
   [EV-COMM-2026-92F1].
3. Indigenous comparison must remain nation-specific; “Indigenous
   storytelling” is too broad to be an engineering pattern
   [EV-COMM-2026-455B].
4. Partial linguistic access may be evidence of border, exile, or institutional
   domination rather than a clarity defect [EV-COMM-2026-9B6C].
5. Translation provenance gates language-level claims across non-U.S. texts
   [EV-COMM-2026-A4F7].

# Open Questions

1. Which dimensions of situated authority can be documented without policing
   identity or creating a reductive score?
2. What community review and permission protocols apply by nation and work?
3. Which functional observations survive comparison across translations?
4. How should multilingual accessibility preserve rather than erase partial
   access and language difference?
5. How do readers from represented communities evaluate extraction and
   distortion differently from outsiders?

# Recommended Next Research

1. Commission a Native/Indigenous literary scholar or community reviewer to
   design a nation-specific protocol study; do not begin with sacred stories.
2. Select one short public-domain or licensed text in two translations and the
   original; record translator decisions before functional comparison.
3. Test functional paraphrase versus contextualized adaptation versus persona
   imitation under community review.

unknown. Recommend reading; do not synthesize a counterfeit composite voice.
